How the data was made

Three weeks, a dozen data sources, and a lot of people called Kumar.

Every chart on this page rests on one claim: that we found the right papers. No database lists all of an institute's papers, so we built the list ourselves and then tested it against papers found another way. These are the steps in order, each with the check that settled it.

  1. 7 Octday 1

    The brief: every paper, nothing guessed

    Collect every publication from 2016 to 2026, with title, authors, year, type, venue, ISSN, DOI, citations and references. Anything we could not establish had to be marked TODO and left visible.

    Why it mattersA gap that is marked can be followed up later. A gap filled with a guess cannot be found again. That rule shaped every later decision.

  2. 7 Octthe first wall

    The obvious source was closed

    OpenAlex, the natural first stop, answered HTTP 429: it now needs an API key, and our shared campus IP had used up the free daily allowance. Scopus needs a key. Semantic Scholar worked only sometimes. We did not try to get around the limits. Crossref worked: a search for the place name Kharagpur returned 13,781 records from 2016 to 2026.

    What we expected, and what happenedWe expected one big database to do most of the work. Instead the dataset had to be built from many smaller sources, each with its own blind spot, and every record had to carry the evidence for why it is in.

  3. 7–8 Octtwo kinds of search

    Search by place, then search by person

    By place: rare words such as city names, PIN codes and misspellings, searched in the affiliations of papers. A classifier decides whether each affiliation string is the IIT, and every string it would not accept was read by a person (78 distinct strings for Kharagpur, 293 for Roorkee).

    By person: Elsevier, the largest publisher in these fields, mostly does not send affiliations to Crossref, so a place search misses many papers. Faculty rosters built from the institute websites and the Wayback Machine (1,144 people for Kharagpur, 849 for Roorkee, including people who left after 2016) gave each person's papers, limited to their years at the IIT.

  4. 8 Octthe referee

    A test set built without any of those tools

    How do you know what you missed? Separate agents built "golden sets" of known papers from faculty pages, news, annual reports and arXiv PDFs, without touching any of the databases the pipeline used: 637 papers for Kharagpur and 562 for Roorkee. Recall is the share of those the pipeline also found.

    Recall went down on purposeIteration 3 found 75% of Kharagpur's golden papers, but the extra papers came from loose name matching. After the precision audit (next step) we removed that matching, and recall fell back to 67% overall, or 81% for papers with a DOI. We kept the lower number because the papers behind it are real.

  5. 8 Octthe audit

    The Kumar problem

    We drew a random sample (fixed seed 20261007) and checked each paper by hand against the affiliation printed on it. Papers whose IIT affiliation was printed on the paper were right 49 times out of 50. Papers found only through a person's name were much worse:

    What we decidedNamesakes caused the mistakes: Kumar, Sharma, Singh, Das and Ghosh are shared by thousands of researchers. Only papers with the IIT printed on them are analysed. The weakest tier (12–22% correct) goes into a separate file that is never analysed, and for 410 common surnames at Kharagpur an initial alone ("N. Kumar") is never matched to a person.

  6. 8–9 Octvalidation

    Zero violations, checked a second way

    An automatic check ran over every record: schema, years, DOI format and uniqueness, ISSNs, references, and a re-run of the classifier on every verified affiliation. Then 200 random records per institute were fetched again live from Crossref.

    0violations, all checks
    99–100%title, year and venue match on live re-fetch
    ρ 0.87–0.88Crossref vs OpenCitations citation counts

    The check found 7 real defects, all fixed in the code rather than patched in the output: 300 records missing metadata, twin DOIs of the same paper (German editions of Angewandte, for example), self-references and supplement DOIs.

  7. 10–12 Octenrichment

    From a list of papers to something you can analyse

    With a key, OpenAlex filled what Crossref lacked: research topics, missing affiliations and the citation links between papers. It only filled gaps. No existing value was overwritten, and every filled value records its source. Journal quartiles came from Scimago and conference ranks from CORE. Papers were linked to faculty by five rules in order of reliability, starting with ORCID: 82.8% of Kharagpur papers and 84.4% of Roorkee papers have at least one faculty member.

    12 Oct: the professor's reviewScope narrowed to science and engineering. Every number was regenerated, the report rewritten, and an explanation added for why the h-index looks low.

  8. 10–28 Octevery number twice

    No number goes in until it is counted a second way

    Each statistic was recomputed by a different route before it went into the report: a matrix count against a loop count, networkx against SciPy, a recount from the submitted CSV, and a second run that had to produce identical files. One such check found eight papers that appeared twice in OpenAlex (online-first year and issue year); Crossref decided which record to keep.

Data analysis

How much do they publish, and is it any good?

Start with counts: papers, journals, fields and the people behind them. Every paper here has the IIT printed on it, is in science or engineering, and was published from 2016 to October 2026.

Kharagpur nearly doubled its output. Roorkee more than doubled.

Kharagpur went from papers in 2016 to in 2025. Roorkee went from to , a steeper climb that took off after 2021.

Not in the numbers

How many professors there were each year. Without yearly faculty lists we cannot tell whether each person writes more, or there are simply more people writing.

Preprints rose from 14 to almost 200 a year at Kharagpur. Part of the "growth" is a change in habit, not only more research.

Two in every three ranked journal papers are in a top-quartile journal.

Scimago ranks journals within their field into four quartiles. Kharagpur puts of its ranked journal papers in Q1 and Roorkee puts there. The very top is small but real: papers sit in journals with an impact factor above 10.

The surprise is who writes those high-impact papers. You might expect large consortia. In fact 92% of them have fewer than ten authors, at both IITs.

Not in the numbers

A quartile ranks the journal, not the paper. A weak paper in a Q1 journal counts as Q1.

Our impact factors are computed from OpenAlex and run higher than the official Clarivate ones (Scientific Reports is 5.2 here and about 3.8 officially).

Engineering first, but water and climate are bigger than you would guess.

Engineering is the largest field at both IITs. At Roorkee, Environmental Science comes second, ahead of Materials and Computer Science. Most of it is water science, hydrology and climate work from the civil engineering, hydrology and water-resources departments, some of Roorkee's oldest strengths.

Not in the numbers

Humanities, management, law and medicine were left out on the professor's instruction. Kharagpur's law school and management school do publish, just not on this page.

Field labels come from a classifier and are not chosen by the authors. Interdisciplinary papers get one label each.

The typical professor has an h-index of 7 or 8 here. Their whole career gives about 20.

The median is 8 at Kharagpur and 7 at Roorkee. An h-index of 8 means eight papers with at least eight citations each. It looks low for an IIT, and it is low on purpose: we count only papers from 2016 on, with this institute's name on them. When we checked against OpenAlex's whole-career profiles, of faculty scored lower here, and the median was about half.

Not in the numbers

Teaching, PhD students trained, patents, policy advice and industry work. The h-index sees only papers that get cited by other papers.

A young professor who joined in 2022 cannot have a high h-index yet, however good their work is.

The most cited papers are often written by very large teams.

Only of Kharagpur's papers have more than ten authors, yet they collect of its citations and six of its ten most cited papers. Take them out and the median faculty h-index drops from 8 to 7.

Not in the numbers

How much one author contributed. A name 30th on a list of 34 counts the same as the first author.

A citation count is also a popularity count. Reviews and software papers (SymPy, iFogSim) collect citations quickly for reasons other than new findings.

Chapter 3: follow the links
3

Network analysis

Who works with whom, and who holds it together?

Two networks: professors linked by papers they wrote together, and papers linked by citations. Here the question is about the shape of the network, not its size.

Two campuses, almost two separate worlds.

Among faculty there are co-author pairs. Only of them cross between Kharagpur and Roorkee. professors have no co-author among the faculty at all, though they still write with students and outside colleagues.

Not in the numbers

Only 7% of a professor's co-author links are with other faculty of these two IITs. Half are with their own students and staff, a fifth with other Indian institutes and a sixth with partners abroad.

A line means "wrote a paper together", which could be anything from a long partnership to a single shared dataset.

Tight little groups, and a small world anyway.

If you shuffle the same number of links at random, almost no triangles appear. The real network has 70 times more "my co-authors also write together" triangles. Groups follow departments only in part: half of all links cross a department boundary.

0.002clustering in random networks of the same size
5.3average handshakes between two connected professors

Not in the numbers

Why the groups form. Shared equipment, a common PhD advisor, a funded project or a hostel friendship all look the same as a line in a graph.

Remove one in fourteen professors, chosen well, and the network falls apart.

Take professors away at random and the network takes the loss calmly: it takes 41% of them to halve the largest connected group. Take away the brokers, the people who sit on the shortest paths between groups, and 7% is enough.

One person matters more than any other between campuses. Removing Pradip K. Maji (Roorkee, Polymer and Process Engineering, six co-authors at Kharagpur) lowers our measure of how easily the two campuses reach each other through co-authors (communicability) by 31%.

Not in the numbers

Whether a broker's collaborators would just find another route. People adapt, and a simulation that deletes nodes does not.

When these papers cite each other, it is almost always the same people.

Among papers of the two IITs, of citations go from a paper to another paper that shares an author. That changes what a "most influential paper" ranking finds. PageRank on this network mostly rewards papers that are old and cited by their own authors' later work. Papers from 2016 are 6.5 times over-represented in its top 1%.

Self-citations also do not predict much. Among papers with the same number of past citations, those whose citations were more often self-citations gained fewer citations later.

Not in the numbers

Self-citation is not cheating. Building on your own last paper is normal. It just means this internal network shows research programmes continuing, not influence spreading.

Citations from the rest of the world are absent here. They predict future citations twice as well (0.75 against 0.38).

They cite India more than India cites them back.

For every 100 citations Kharagpur gives to other Indian institutes, it receives back. Roorkee receives .

That ten-year total hides a turn. In 2016 nobody could yet cite papers that did not exist, so the early years are all outflow. From 2023 at Kharagpur and 2024 at Roorkee, other Indian institutes cite them more each year than they cite those institutes.

Not in the numbers

Why a citation was made. Several of the top citers are large private universities whose output grew very fast. A citation from them counts the same as one from IISc.

Pairs hide the teams, and teams grow along old friendships.

A paper with 30 authors creates 435 pairs at once. Papers with more than ten authors are only 1.8% of the papers but create 27% of all author pairs. So we also looked at whole teams.

Three researchers who have each written in pairs, but never as a trio, form a trio later 3.7% of the time. Three with no earlier links do so about 3 times in a billion. Co-authors also cite each other: 14% of co-author pairs do, against 0.11% of pairs who are far apart in the network.

Not in the numbers

Which open triangles will close. Our best predictor barely beats a coin toss (0.56). We can see that trios form along old links, but not which trio will form next.

Teams that mix departments get no citation bonus once team size is fixed (ratio 1.006), against a common belief.

Chapter 4: the blind spots
4

Blind spots

What the numbers miss.

Each limit below comes with the number that measures it. Read the earlier chapters with these in mind.

1 in 5

Papers we know exist but did not find

Against the golden sets, about 81% of papers with a DOI were found. Most misses are Elsevier and IEEE papers by faculty with very common names, where no open source shows the affiliation.

1 in 6

Papers with no professor attached

7,587 of the 46,071 papers could not be linked to anyone on the faculty rosters: work by students, postdocs and staff, or by faculty the rosters missed. They count for the institute, but not for any person in the people charts or the games.

½

Of a career, in the h-index

Ten years and one institute only. A professor who did their best-known work elsewhere looks smaller here than in Google Scholar.

10 months

Of 2026

The data was collected in October 2026. Recent papers also have had little time to gather citations, so every per-year citation number favours older work.

0

Teaching, students, patents, policy

A professor who trains 20 PhD students, or whose work changed a national water policy, can look ordinary in a citation count.

88%

Of internal citations are self-citations

The citation network between these papers mostly shows people continuing their own work, not one group influencing another.

1 count

Per author, whatever they did

The 30th of 34 authors gets the same credit as the first. Big-team papers move headline numbers more than their share of papers suggests.

≠

A quartile is not a verdict

Q1 and impact factor describe the journal. Our impact factors run higher than the official ones, so compare them within this page, not with outside numbers.

Keep those limits in mind, then try the data yourself: can you guess which paper has more citations, or which professor would win a head-to-head?
